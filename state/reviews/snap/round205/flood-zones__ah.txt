Flood zone AH: meaning, who must insure, cost ($874 median a year) Flood Insurance Data Flood zone AH: what it means, who must insure, what it costs Zone AH is a high-risk area of shallow flooding: a 1 percent or greater annual chance of flooding to an average depth of 1 to 3 feet, where FEMA has worked out how high the water would reach. Such flooding is characterized by ponding or sheet flow (44 CFR §59.1, §64.3). A 1 percent or greater chance a year works out to a 26% or greater chance of at least one such flood over 30 years. Must I buy? Federal law requires it only in a high-risk zone (A or V) with a federally regulated or backed mortgage, or federal help to buy or build (42 U.S.C. §4012a). Outside those zones, a lender may still require it. Buying, refinancing or questioning the zone Buying a finished home with an NFIP building policy: the seller can assign the policy to you in writing when title transfers. An owner or renter who believes the property was put in a high-risk A or V zone by mistake, because of how the zone line was drawn against streets or other features, can send FEMA technical information and ask for a Letter of Map Amendment, which identifies the property to be excluded from the zone (44 CFR part 70). FEMA charges no review or processing fee for a Letter of Map Amendment request (44 CFR §72.5). FEMA's Online LOMC site lets home owners or their representatives ask FEMA for a Letter of Map Change. If you think a lender's high-risk-zone finding is wrong, you and the lender together can ask FEMA to review it within 45 days of the lender's notice; a fee applies (44 CFR §65.17). In zones AE, AH and VE, FEMA has worked out the water surface elevation of the base flood: the flood level that has a 1 percent or greater chance of occurrence in any given year (44 CFR §59.1, §64.3). Where FEMA has published final flood elevations, a community in the NFIP must require new homes, and homes being substantially improved, in zones AE and AH to have the lowest floor, including the basement, at or above the base flood level, unless FEMA has granted the community a basement exception (44 CFR §60.3(c)(2)). After a flood: Under the NFIP's rules, a building is substantially damaged when restoring it to its condition before the damage would cost 50 percent or more of its market value before the damage (44 CFR §59.1). An NFIP policy with building coverage pays up to $30,000 under Increased Cost of Compliance to comply with a state or local floodplain law when repairing a flood-damaged building. Your home may already have an Elevation Certificate on file with your community: ask your local floodplain manager, often in the city or county building, permitting or engineering department. If none is on file, a land surveyor, engineer or architect completes one. Federal flood insurance (NFIP) is sold only in communities that take part in the NFIP; in a community that has not joined, or once a suspension takes effect, no one can buy an NFIP policy. Will it rise, and can I pay less? FEMA prices each building from where it is (distance to the coast, rivers and other flood sources, and how high the ground sits), how it is built (foundation type, first-floor height) and what the policy covers (coverage amounts and deductibles). By law, most premium rates may not rise more than 18% a year (42 U.S.C. §4015(e)). The exceptions are in the NFIP rules on premium increases . FEMA's ways that may lower an NFIP bill: a higher deductible (you pay more of any loss), work that lowers the building's flood risk, and an Elevation Certificate. How do I get covered? Most policies start 30 days after you buy. Bought while making, increasing, extending or renewing a mortgage, it can start at closing if the insurer is paid within 10 days (30 if the lender, title company or settlement attorney pays). To buy: call the NFIP at (877) 336-2627 , go to FloodSmart.gov , or ask any licensed insurance agent. Every NFIP insurer offers the same rates.
