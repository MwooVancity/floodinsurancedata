Federal law requires it only in a high-risk zone (A or V) with a federally regulated or backed mortgage, or federal help to buy or build (42 U.S.C. §4012a). Outside those zones, a lender may still require it. A private flood policy can meet a lender's flood requirement: federally regulated lenders must accept a private policy that meets the federal definition of private flood insurance and covers at least what the loan requires (12 CFR §339.3(c)). Its price is not in FEMA's records. At a closing: Where a federally regulated lender must require it, the legal minimum is the loan balance or the NFIP's limit for the property, whichever is less (42 U.S.C. §4012a(b)). If such a lender notifies you that coverage is needed and you do not buy it within 45 days, the lender must buy it for you and can charge you for it (42 U.S.C. §4012a(e)). If you think a lender's high-risk-zone finding is wrong, you and the lender together can ask FEMA to review it within 45 days of the lender's notice; a fee applies (44 CFR §65.17). Buying a finished home with an NFIP building policy: the seller can assign the policy to you in writing when title transfers. 93.2% of Greene County's NFIP policy units are in high-risk zones (not 93.2% of all buildings, and not an answer about your own home). Data effective August 31, 2021 to August 31, 2026; retrieved November 11, 2026. How do I actually get covered — and how fast? Most policies start 30 days after you buy. Bought while making, increasing, extending or renewing a mortgage, it can start at closing if the insurer is paid within 10 days (30 if the lender, title company or settlement attorney pays). Waiting period 30 days Bought with a new or refinanced mortgage Can start at closing, if paid in time NFIP building limit $250,000 NFIP contents limit $100,000 Most homeowners insurance does not cover flood damage. Exceptions in FEMA's flood insurance manual: with a loan secured by the insured property (making, increasing, extending or renewing it), coverage you apply for on or before closing can start at the time of the loan closing if the insurer gets the form and full amount due in time; the wait is one day when a FEMA map revision newly shows the building in a high-risk zone and the insurer gets them within 13 months of the revision's effective date. One more exception (44 CFR 61.11(c)): coverage starts the day after you apply and pay when FEMA determines the property is affected by flooding on federal land caused or made worse by post-wildfire conditions, and you buy it within 60 days after the fire's containment date. To buy: call the NFIP at (877) 336-2627 , go to FloodSmart.gov , or ask any licensed insurance agent. Every NFIP insurer offers the same rates. The program nationwide: the NFIP in numbers . Congress has to keep renewing the NFIP's authority. During a lapse, NFIP policies cannot be bought, renewed or increased until the authority returns. Policies already in force run to their expiration date and claims on them are still handled, though FEMA says the money to pay claims may be limited. What it won't pay: The policy generally counts a flood only when water covers two or more acres of normally dry land or two or more properties, one of them yours. The NFIP's standard policy for homes (the Dwelling Form) does not pay for a hotel or other living costs while you are out of the home, or for belongings outside the building, and it limits what it pays for in a basement. Belongings are paid at actual cash value (their used value), not the cost of new ones. Hurricanes can cause both wind and flood damage. A standard NFIP policy does not pay for wind damage, including rain blown in by wind or water coming through a roof the wind damaged; it does pay for water entering from the ground up, such as storm surge. Other policies, such as homeowners policies, may cover wind-driven rain, so you will likely file claims with both. Already flooded? Insured: report the loss to your insurance agent or company now (FEMA's steps: how to start a flood claim ), and send a proof of loss (your signed, sworn statement of the amount you are claiming) within 60 days of the flood (44 CFR 61, App. A(1), Art. VII). Not insured: a policy bought today will not cover a flood that already happened, and FEMA disaster aid is open to people in counties designated in a federally declared disaster. If FEMA aid after a flood goes to buy or build a home in a high-risk zone, you must buy and keep flood insurance on it for at least the amount of the aid, or you cannot get federal aid for later flood damage to it (44 CFR §206.110). NFIP claim denied? You can appeal to FEMA within 60 days of the date on the denial letter, and you can sue within one year of the denial. An appeal does not extend that year. Under the NFIP's rules, a building is substantially damaged when restoring it to its condition before the damage would cost 50 percent or more of its market value before the damage (44 CFR §59.1). An NFIP policy with building coverage pays up to $30,000 under Increased Cost of Compliance to comply with a state or local floodplain law when repairing a flood-damaged building. Median premium charged (before fees) $559 Median full-risk premium $664 Pay below full-risk 53.7% of 205 Arkansas: 41.4% Yearly cap: Except for the Emergency Program discount, statutory discounts gradually phase out through an annual increase cap discount at each renewal date until the policy reaches its full-risk premium. By law, most premium rates may not rise more than 18% a year; the rate for some property, including homes that are not a primary residence and business property, rises 25% a year until that group's average rate reaches the level the law sets (42 U.S.C. §4015(e)). FloodSmart puts the limit at 18 to 25% a year for most policyholders. It usually does not apply to new policies, raising your coverage or lowering your deductible can go past it, and fees and surcharges are added on top. Paying a renewal 30 days or more after the policy expires causes a lapse in coverage. You then need a new application, and the lapse may cost you statutory discounts (what keeps a bill below full risk), including the yearly cap. Source: FEMA OpenFEMA — NFIP Policies.
