Did FEMA aid homeowners after floods and storms? Are these counts complete? Am I required to buy it? Federal law requires it only in a high-risk zone (A or V) with a federally regulated or backed mortgage, or federal help to buy or build (42 U.S.C. §4012a). Outside them, a lender can still require it. A private flood policy can meet a lender's flood requirement: federally regulated lenders must accept a private policy that meets the federal definition of private flood insurance and covers at least what the loan requires (12 CFR §339.3(c)). Its price is not in FEMA's records. At a closing: Where a federally regulated lender must require it, the legal minimum is the loan balance or the NFIP's limit for the property, whichever is less (42 U.S.C. §4012a(b)). If such a lender notifies you that coverage is needed and you do not buy it within 45 days, the lender must buy it for you and can charge you for it (42 U.S.C. §4012a(e)). If you think a lender's high-risk-zone finding is wrong, you and the lender together can ask FEMA to review it within 45 days of the lender's notice; a fee applies, and FEMA checks only the zone finding, not your flood risk (44 CFR §65.17). Buying a home with an NFIP policy: the seller can assign the policy to you in writing when title transfers, and FEMA says a below-full-risk discount can move to the new owner that way. 51.0% of Louisiana's NFIP policy units are in high-risk zones (not 51.0% of all buildings, and not an answer about your own home). Most policies start 30 days after you buy, but there is no wait if you buy it with a new or refinanced mortgage. Waiting period 30 days Bought with a new or refinanced mortgage No wait NFIP building limit $250,000 NFIP contents limit $100,000 Standard homeowners insurance does not cover flood damage; flood insurance is bought separately, through the federal NFIP or a private insurer. Exceptions: no wait if you buy while making, increasing, extending or renewing a mortgage; a one-day wait if a map revision puts the property in a high-risk zone and you buy within 13 months of the revision (44 CFR §61.11). To buy: call the NFIP at (877) 336-2627 , go to FloodSmart.gov , or ask any licensed insurance agent. Every NFIP insurer offers the same rates. Congress has to keep renewing the NFIP's authority. During a lapse, NFIP policies cannot be bought, renewed or increased until the authority returns. Policies already in force run to their expiration date and claims on them are still handled, though FEMA says the money to pay claims may be limited. What it won't pay: The policy generally counts a flood only when water covers two or more acres of normally dry land or two or more properties, one of them yours. A standard NFIP home policy does not pay for a hotel or other living costs while you are out of the home, or for belongings outside the building, and it limits what it pays for in a basement. Belongings are paid at actual cash value (their used value), not the cost of new ones. Already flooded? Insured: report the loss to your insurance agent or company now (FEMA's steps: how to start a flood claim ), and send a proof of loss (your signed, sworn statement of the amount you are claiming) within 60 days of the flood (44 CFR 61, App. A(1), Art. VII). Not insured: a policy bought today will not cover a flood that already happened, and FEMA disaster aid is open to people in counties designated in a federally declared disaster. If FEMA aid pays to repair a home in a high-risk zone, you must buy and keep flood insurance on it, or you cannot get that aid again after a later flood. NFIP claim denied? You can appeal to FEMA within 60 days of the date on the denial letter, and you can sue within one year of the denial. An appeal does not extend that year. If repairs would cost 50 percent or more of the building's market value before the flood, it counts as substantially damaged under federal rules, and local floodplain rules can require elevating or other work. On an NFIP policy with building coverage, Increased Cost of Compliance pays up to $30,000 toward that; the local official's letter has to come before the work starts. Flooded? Work out the proof-of-loss deadline for an NFIP (National Flood Insurance Program) policy. If the President declares a major disaster or emergency for your area, FEMA's standard window to register for disaster aid is 60 days from that declaration date, and FEMA can extend it (44 CFR §206.112). A private (non-NFIP) flood policy sets its own claim deadlines; they are in that policy. How do current premiums compare with FEMA's full-risk price? 56.6% of policies here pay less than FEMA's full-risk price, so their bills are rising toward it. Median premium charged (before fees) $859 Median full-risk premium $1,220 Pay below full-risk 56.6% of 292,018 Yearly cap: Those prices step up toward full-risk each year, limited by the yearly cap. By law a primary home's premium cannot rise more than 18% a year; some policies, such as second homes and businesses, can rise up to 25%. The cap usually does not apply to new policies, and fees and surcharges sit outside it. Bills already at full risk still change with FEMA's yearly rate updates. An NFIP policy paid 30 days or more after it expires cannot be renewed; it takes a new application, and FEMA says the lapse may affect its statutory discounts (what keeps a bill below full risk).
