# Publish the county numbers nobody has ranked

The flood stories that spread from 2018 to 2026 all used the same recipe: one surprising number set against what people assume or what FEMA says, cut by county so every local paper could run its own version, and published within days of a named flood. Almost every national angle is already taken: claims outside high-risk zones, grants vs insurance payouts, repeatedly flooded homes, old maps, the NFIP debt, the Helene coverage gap. What is still open is the **county-level version of those angles**, plus four claim-field angles where the research found no coverage at all: **why claims were not paid, the map changing under the claim, damage vs payout, and "it wasn't a river"**. These need no outside data, come straight from FEMA's claims file, and answer the reader question the site already asks ("what floods pay here"). Ranked first are the ones that are new, strictly true at the unit shown, and easy to localize. Last are the ones to never publish because the data cannot carry them. One warning on the evidence: no figure in this report has been computed from raw data yet. Every number is quoted from the linked source, most were seen only in search summaries, and "no coverage found" rests on about 15 searches, not proof. Each figure must be recomputed from OpenFEMA before it goes on a page.

## Three stories spread for the same three reasons

**First Street's 2020 launch** said 14.6 million properties face 1-in-100-year flood risk against 8.7 million in FEMA zones. It was a young nonprofit's first release, and it still landed on CBS, Fox and USA Today, with local cuts in Ohio and elsewhere ([CBS](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/15-million-homes-at-risk-of-flooding-new-data-from-first-street-foundation/); [Route Fifty](https://www.route-fifty.com/management/2020/07/millions-homeowners-who-need-flood-insurance-dont-know-fema/166634)). It worked because it contradicted an authority, gave every metro its own number, and offered a free lookup. **After Helene in 2024**, NBC, Reuters and the Washington Post each ran county flood-insurance take-up within about a week of landfall: about 2% of residences in the 100 hardest-hit counties ([NBC News](https://www.nbcnews.com/data-graphics/map-flood-insurance-coverage-counties-hit-hurricane-helene-rcna173619)), 0.8% inland vs 21% on the coast, and **Mitchell County with 31 policies for 8,600+ housing units** ([ADN/Washington Post](https://www.adn.com/nation-world/2024/10/03/nearly-all-homes-in-counties-hardest-hit-by-helene-lack-national-flood-insurance/)). NBC's map used NFIP data "collected by Neptune Flood", a private insurer, which shows a third party with county numbers ready gets named. **NRDC's repetitive-loss dashboard** spread through one vivid house in Batchelor, Louisiana that flooded 40 times and was paid $428,379 ([Insurance Journal](https://www.insurancejournal.com/news/national/2024/09/19/793586.htm)).

The repeated stats share one shape: a share near zero, a "times" count, or a dollar total, each needing no table. Several of the most repeated were also badly sourced. The Harvey "80% uninsured" figure was one ex-commissioner's estimate, while data-based counts were 16-17% insured ([WNYC](https://wnycstudios.org/story/why-80-percent-hurricane-harvey-victims-dont-have-flood-insurance); [Insurance Journal 2018](https://insurancejournal.com/magazines/mag-features/2018/08/06/496796.htm)). Helene coverage figures ranged from 0.5% to 5.2% depending on what the share was taken of ([Carrier Management](https://www.carriermanagement.com/news/2024/10/03/267204.htm); [NC Newsline](https://ncnewsline.com/2025/04/22/few-were-insured-against-helenes-floods-those-that-were-faced-long-claims-frustrating-hurdles/)). That is the opening for a site whose rule is never to guess: **be the exact, dated, sourced number reporters can cite instead of the round scary one.** The research found no case of a brand-new anonymous site winning a national link from flood data. The small winners were nonprofits, insurers and local newsrooms.

Three stories should never be published. **"Who pays in vs who gets paid"** by state or county: premium data starts years after claims data (roughly 2009 vs 1978), premium is not total cost, and one storm like Katrina dominates, so the windows cannot be matched honestly ([Wharton](https://esg.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2023/08/WRCib2011b-nfip-who-pays.pdf) did it only for 1978-2008). **"Deepest flood" by water depth**: FEMA says inches with some records in feet, while a Nature Communications study treated the field as feet with about 5% in inches ([Nature Comms](https://www.Nature.Com/articles/s41467-020-15264-2)), so any extreme is unsafe. **"Worst county" composite scores**: those drew backlash every time they were tried ([AJC](https://www.ajc.com/news/local-govt--politics/metro-atlanta-cities-push-back-after-being-named-worst-cities/8grTxIOfSK5Ji7arnRdr6K)). NOAA sea level paired with policy decline is already taken ("flood insurance is vanishing where the water is rising fastest", [Insurance Business](https://www.insurancebusinessmag.com/us/news/catastrophe/flood-insurance-is-vanishing-where-the-water-is-rising-fastest-591837.aspx)). The NFIP's $22.5 billion debt ([FEMA](https://www.fema.gov/print/pdf/node/692081)) is national context only and cannot be cut by county.

One caveat applies to stories 1, 2, 3, 5, 6 and 9: the claims county field is "the primary county associated with the project" and "may not match the property's exact county" ([Claims v3](https://www.fema.gov/openfema-data-page/nfip-redacted-claims-v3)). Every county claims figure needs that line printed beside it.

## A county map plus a lookup beats any one-off report

No independent study ranks formats by links; the evidence is agency case studies. Within that, Fractl's review of its top 50 campaigns found **filled maps and images did best**, and traditional charts, videos and infographics "trailed significantly" ([Fractl](https://www.frac.tl/?p=10976)). Rankings of every place on one figure work because each place becomes its own local story ([BuzzStream](https://www.buzzstream.com/?p=7915)). A typical campaign earns links from about 42 domains ([Go Fish Digital](https://gofishdigital.com/blog/digital-pr-increase-online-authority/)), and data pages keep earning: one went from 7 links at launch to 119+ referring domains later ([Go Fish Digital](https://gofishdigital.com/blog/how-to-build-evergreen-digital-pr-campaigns-that-keep-earning-links/)). That fits the site as built: each story should be **one national page with a county map, a full ranked table and a CSV**, with the county figure also shown on each county page so the lookup does the localizing.

Timing decides disaster citations. Reactive-PR guidance puts the window at hours, commonly 2-6 ([BuzzStream](https://buzzstream.com/?p=8375)), and Google News Lab found 49% of data stories are made in a day or less ([Google](https://www.blog.google/outreach-initiatives/google-news-initiative/data-journalism-2017/)). Helene made landfall 26 September 2024 and the county analyses ran 3-7 October. **A page built after landfall is usually too late; the one already indexed gets found.** Redfin shows the repeatable version: it re-times releases to live floods, such as its Skagit County figure during the December 2025 Washington atmospheric river ([Redfin](https://www.redfin.com/news/press-releases/washington-flooding-redfins-latest-flood-risk-and-housing-data-as-atmospheric-river-sweeps-state/)).

## An anonymous site earns trust through method, not a name

No survey asks whether journalists cite anonymous sites, so this is inference. What the surveys do show is that accuracy is a rising worry: 50% of reporters call fighting misinformation a top challenge, up from 40% ([PR Daily on Cision 2026](https://www.prdaily.com/data-experts-and-early-access-what-journalists-want-in-2026/)), and 55% want original research ([Cision](https://www.cision.co.uk/resources/articles/pr-lessons-state-of-the-media/)). One editor deletes any ranking that does not clearly explain its method ([Times of San Diego](https://timesofsandiego.com/?p=250815)). The data newsletter Data Is Plural takes free submissions but requires bulk download, documentation of method and known issues, a clear licence and a stable URL ([Data Is Plural](https://www.data-is-plural.com/faq/criteria/)). Put together, the stand-in for a named author is **the federal source and as-of date on every figure, a methodology section listing the traps (county caveat, excluded codes, denominators), a CSV per story, a stable URL per county, a licence line and a role-based contact email**. Pitches go 1:1 by email, under 200 words, before noon ([PR News on Muck Rack 2025](https://www.prnewsonline.com/the-state-of-journalism-in-2025-insights-to-optimize-your-media-relations/)), with one exact figure and its base. Quote-request services like HARO and Qwoted expect a named expert, so they suit this site poorly; Data Is Plural and direct pitches suit it well. Paid or swapped links are spam under Google's rules ([Google](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/essentials/spam-policies#link-spam)).

The flood-specific warning is First Street. Its scores drew a Florida attorney-general lawsuit, homeowner suits and an MLS push that got Zillow to pull them off listings in late 2025 ([ESG Dive](https://www.esgdive.com/news/florida-ag-sues-climate-analytics-provider-first-street-over-home-risk-scor/873473/); [MassLandlords](https://masslandlords.net/zillow-removes-vital-climate-risk-info-on-real-estate-listings/)). The backlash hit **modelled, property-level precision**. Counted county totals from FEMA's own files, with the base stated, are the opposite kind of claim, and that difference is this site's edge.

## Conclusion

The shock in this field does not need inventing. FEMA's own claims file already holds counter-intuitive, never-ranked county facts: why claims closed unpaid, how often today's map disagrees with the zone a policy was rated on, and how often flooding came from pooled rain rather than a river. The competition, First Street, Neptune and national desks, either models risk (and draws lawsuits) or recomputes take-up from scratch on deadline. A site that keeps those county figures exact, dated and downloadable *before* the next storm can be the source reporters cite, and the "always print the base and what the data cannot say" rule becomes the selling point rather than a constraint.

The biggest unknown is whether an anonymous site gets credited at all. No evidence answers it either way. The cheapest test is story 1 or 2: build it once from claims fields the site already loads, submit the CSV to Data Is Plural, and see whether it is picked up before the next hurricane season.
